# On the Birth and Death of the Lesser Gods
:octicons-info-24:{ .lg .middle } **A [Dunmari](<../../gazetteer/greater-dunmar/realms/dunmar/dunmar.md>) theological treatise, written in the DR 1670s, by Esa**  

*On the Birth and Death of the Lesser Gods* is a work by Esa, a Lakan scholar who wrote in the 1670s. Esa examines minor divinities that seem to arise and disappear, comparing several traditions that Dunmari theology groups uneasily under the category of lesser gods. Much of the work concerns a group of deities often linked in [Dunmari religious traditions](<../../gods-and-religions/religions/five-siblings/dunmari-religion.md>): [Bhishma](<../../gods-and-religions/gods/incorporeal-gods/dunmari-pantheon/bhishma.md>), [Shakun](<../../gods-and-religions/gods/incorporeal-gods/dunmari-pantheon/shakun.md>), and [Adra](<../../gods-and-religions/gods/incorporeal-gods/dunmari-pantheon/adra.md>). In classical Dunmari teaching, these deities are believed to have  received divinity from [the Five Siblings](<../../gods-and-religions/religions/five-siblings/dunmari-religion.md>), who bestowed part of their power upon those who would otherwise have remained mortal.

Esa, however, is interested in comparative practical theology, and seeks to place Dunmari tradition the context of three other traditions that are less easily classified: the [Kestavo](<../../gods-and-religions/religions/northern-folk-religions/kestavo.md>) of [Sembara](<../../gazetteer/greater-sembara/sembara/sembara.md>), the [tanshi](<../../gods-and-religions/gods/tanshi/tanshi.md>) of the northern [Deno'qai](<../../groups/cultures/deno-qai-tribes/deno-qai.md>), and the kadaa, or saints, of the [Mawar](<../../gods-and-religions/religions/mos-numena/mawaran-religion.md>). Most of her work concerns long discussions of the theology and reality of these traditions, from a Dunmari perspective. 

The kestavo are largely dismissed. Esa proposes that some of these, or perhaps many of them, are in fact fey mistaken for gods, much like the like the fey guardians associated with [Tyrwingha](<../../gazetteer/greater-sembara/tyrwingha/tyrwingha.md>). The kadaa, the saints of the the [Mawar](<../../gods-and-religions/religions/mos-numena/mawaran-religion.md>), she discusses only briefly. These deities, she writes, are simply mortals raised to divinity by the [Eight Divines](<../../gods-and-religions/gods/incorporeal-gods/mos-numena-pantheon/mos-numena-pantheon.md>) of the [Mos Numena](<../../gods-and-religions/religions/mos-numena/mos-numena.md>) tradition, directly analogous [Bhishma](<../../gods-and-religions/gods/incorporeal-gods/dunmari-pantheon/bhishma.md>) in the Dunmari tradition. 

The tanshi, which occupy much of the text, present Esa with a different problem. She accepts them as genuine gods, yet observes that many appear to have vanished as their worshippers were dispersed or destroyed. Drawing on accounts of the Drankorian conquest of the north and later devastation during the [Great War](<../../events/1500s/great-war.md>), the [Blood Years](<../../events/1500s/blood-years.md>), and the hobgoblin wars, she asks whether a god can truly die when the community sustaining its connection to the world is destroyed. Esa argues that divine energy cannot itself be destroyed. A god whose worship disappears should therefore be understood not as dead but as severed: a surviving divinity whose connection to the world has faded away. If belief creates or sustains that connection, she reasons, renewed worship might restore it. The work consequently imagines lost divinities wandering in some unknown realm beyond the [Divine Veil](<../../cosmology/land-of-the-dead.md>), cut off from Taelgar but potentially capable of return.
